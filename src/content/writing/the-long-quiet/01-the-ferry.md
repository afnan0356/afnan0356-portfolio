The ferry left at six, before the light had decided what it wanted to be. There were four of us
on the deck and a boy selling tea from a kettle balanced on a paraffin stove.

I had been told the crossing took forty minutes. It took an hour and ten, and nobody on board
seemed surprised by this, least of all the boy, who sold all his tea by the second bend and then
sat with his back against the rail, watching the water the way you watch a fire.

The river here is wide enough that the far bank is a rumour. You see a line of trees, a few roofs,
the pale smear of a road, and you understand that you are looking at distance the way you look at
a photograph of a mountain — flat, and smaller than the truth.

A man in a grey shirt asked me where I was going. I told him the name of the village, and he
nodded as if I had said something obvious.

"That's where everybody goes," he said, and went back to his own thoughts.

I did not have a return ticket. I had a bag with a change of clothes, a notebook, and a letter
that I had read so many times the folds had gone soft. I had told myself I was going there to
settle a matter of property. It would be more honest to say that I was going there because I had
run out of reasons to stay anywhere else.

The engine changed pitch. The boy got up and went to the rail, and the far bank began, very
slowly, to become a place.
