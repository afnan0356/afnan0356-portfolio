The house was smaller than the letter had implied. Letters do that; they write in a larger hand
than the world uses.

It stood at the end of a lane that was mostly mud and, in one fifty-yard stretch, entirely bricks —
someone's abandoned improvement, half finished, the gaps between the bricks filled with grass. A
mango tree leaned over the gate as though it had been asked to keep the place upright.

There was a woman on the veranda. She was maybe sixty, wearing a green shawl against a cold that
I could not feel, and she watched me come up the lane without any expression at all, the way you
watch weather.

"You're late," she said, when I was close enough.

"The ferry was slow."

"The ferry is always slow." She stood, and the chair she had been in rocked once behind her and
settled. "You should have come in the morning. People come in the morning."

I said that I had come as soon as I could. She considered this, and then she nodded, once, as
though I had passed a small test I had not known I was taking, and went inside to put a kettle on.

The door did not close properly behind her. I stood in the yard, holding my bag, and listened to
the house settling — the small wooden conversation of a building that has been left alone with
itself for a long time.
