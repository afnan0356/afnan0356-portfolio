The tools I have used longest are the ones I have thought about least. A text editor whose
shortcuts live in my fingers. A note-taking program that is a folder. A calculator that does not
want to be a spreadsheet.

None of them were exciting on the day I installed them. That may be the entire mechanism. A small
tool does not ask you to reorganise your work around it; it does one thing, gets out of the way,
and — crucially — never demands to be upgraded.

## The cost of being interesting

Big software earns attention, and attention has to be paid for. That usually means a surface where
the product can remind you it exists: notifications, streaks, a weekly summary of how much you
have been using the thing you are using. Those features are not malicious. They are the interest
payments on the capital that built the tool.

Small software has no such obligation because it has no such capital. Somebody wrote it because
they needed it. That is a different kind of guarantee, and it tends to outlast a roadmap.

## What this means for building

If you build something for other people, the most respectful thing you can do is make it
finishable. A tool that can be understood completely, in an afternoon, by the person using it, is a
better gift than a platform that will be improved every quarter for a decade and owned by nobody
in particular.

> Make the thing small enough that someone can hold all of it in their head.

That is not a rule about lines of code. It is a rule about expectations.
